**TL;DR** — The hardest part of building solo isn't execution, it's signal. Three passes turn a flood of newly launched startup projects into a shortlist worth a solo founder's attention:

- **Watch what just launched.** A live project is the market voting with real effort. Read it while the noise is still low.
- **Read a lane's density before you enter it.** Crowding confirms demand — it tells you to hunt for a seam, not to walk away.
- **Judge replication difficulty.** An opportunity can be real and still be wrong for one person.

Run the three in order and "what should I build?" turns from a guessing game into a weekly habit.

## Who this is for, and where it stops

This is written for solo founders and independent makers who can ship but haven't picked a direction — people with more build capacity than conviction. Most of them don't lack ideas; they lack signal about which ideas are real, early, and small enough for one person to carry.

This playbook covers **discovery**: surfacing candidates and cutting them down to a shortlist. It stops there. Validating demand, pricing, and launch are separate jobs — the [competitor research playbook](/playbooks/research-competitors) picks up where this one ends.

## Path 1 — Watch what just launched

You don't need to predict the future. New startup projects go live constantly, and each one is somebody betting real effort on a real problem. The edge is timing: read the signal while the noise is still low and before the lane fills in.

The hard part was never finding projects — far more go live than any one person could track by hand, and almost all of them blur together. What helps is the smaller set that rises to the top once you weigh how realistically one person could actually build it. The projects below come from that set: early, still forming, and already showing the shape of something a solo founder can study.

## Path 3 — Use replication difficulty to judge if it's solo-friendly

An opportunity can be hot and still be wrong for one person. If shipping it needs a team, capital, or a license you don't have, it's a trap no matter how validated the market looks.

That's why each project carries a replication-difficulty read:
- **Low** — one person can ship a weekend prototype
- **Medium** — doable solo, but slower; expect weeks, not days
- **High** — needs a resource moat: a team, funding, licensing, or proprietary supply

The projects that land in the low tier are the ones actually within reach of a solo founder — worth a closer look before anything else. The card below is a reference point from a crowded lane: an AI tool at *medium* difficulty, useful for calibrating what "harder" looks like.

## Where this filter misleads you

Three passes are a shortlist machine, not an oracle. The failure modes are worth knowing before you lean on it:

- **Early looks the same as abandoned.** A project that launched recently and then went quiet reads identically to one that's still forming. Give a candidate a second look a few weeks later before concluding anything from its first appearance.
- **Low difficulty cuts both ways.** If one person can rebuild it in a weekend, so can everyone else who noticed. Low difficulty tells you the door is open; it says nothing about how long it stays open. Pair it with a lane where you have an unfair angle — an audience, a domain you know, a distribution channel already warm.
- **The filter only sees what ships.** Opportunities that live inside companies, in offline workflows, or in communities that never register a domain are invisible here. This is a strong signal for software, and a blind spot for everything else.

Treat a shortlist as the beginning of research, not the end of it.

## FAQ

### How do I know which new projects are worth my time?

Recency plus reach. A project that went live recently still has a readable story — you can see what the founder chose to build first. Reach is the second half: if rebuilding it would need a team or capital you don't have, the insight is interesting but not actionable. Worth your time means both, at once.

### What makes a project solo-friendly?

A narrow job, a small surface area, and no dependency you can't supply yourself. Solo-friendly projects tend to avoid marketplaces (two sides to bootstrap), regulated data, and anything needing enterprise sales. The shortest test: could you describe the whole product in one sentence, and does that sentence name exactly one job?

### Isn't a crowded niche a reason to walk away?

Usually the opposite. Crowding means somebody has already proven people pay for the outcome, which removes the single riskiest unknown. Treat density as a signal to narrow — serve a segment the broad players ignore — rather than a signal to leave. The lanes to avoid are the ones that are quiet for a reason.

### How often should this be done?

Weekly is enough, and more often adds little. New projects surface continuously, but the ones worth studying need a little time to show whether they're still alive and still moving. A weekly pass catches them early without reacting to every blip.

### What if I already have an idea?

Run it through pass three first. If your idea sits in the high tier, you've learned something cheap and early. If it's low or medium, use pass two to see who else is in that lane — that's the fastest way to find out whether you're early or late, and it's the input the [competitor research playbook](/playbooks/research-competitors) needs.
